What is Linear Regression? Drawing the fairest straight line through data to predict the future.

Imagine looking at 12 apartments in Hong Kong and plotting their size against monthly rent. The dots don't form a perfect line, but there is a clear upward trend. Linear Regression is the machine learning method that finds the single best-fitting straight line through those dots—so you can estimate the price of a brand-new apartment you've never seen before.

ŷ = w · x + b
ŷ (Prediction)
The machine's estimated answer (e.g., predicted rent).
x (Feature / Input)
The clue you already know (e.g., flat size in sq.ft).
w (Weight / Slope)
How steep the line is: how much ŷ changes when x goes up by 1.
b (Bias / Intercept)
The starting baseline value when x is zero.
Everyday Taxi Analogy: A taxi meter is literally a linear regression formula! The flag-fall starting fare ($29) is the Bias (b), the price per kilometer ($10/km) is the Weight (w), the distance traveled is Input (x), and the total fare is Prediction (ŷ).

6 Essential Concepts Behind Linear Regression

You don't need advanced calculus to understand how machines learn from numbers. Every linear regression model revolves around these six simple ideas.

01 · x & y

Feature (x) vs. Target (y)

In Machine Learning, a Feature (x) is the input clue you measure, and the Target (y) is the real-world outcome you want to predict. During training, we show the computer historical pairs of (x, y).

Example: Input x = Daily temperature (32°C). Target y = Actual cups of iced coffee sold (210 cups).
02 · w & b

Weight (w) & Bias (b)

Think of w and b as the two tuning knobs inside the machine. Weight (w) controls the tilt (how much each extra unit of x adds or subtracts), while Bias (b) shifts the entire line up or down.

Negative Weight: If w is negative (e.g., car age vs. price), the line slopes downward—older cars cost less!
03 · y − ŷ

Residuals (Prediction Errors)

Real life is messy, so almost no dot sits perfectly on the line. The vertical gap between an actual dot (y) and the line's prediction (ŷ) is called a Residual or Error: e = y − ŷ.

Visual Cue: Turn on "Error Lines" in the chart above—those dashed vertical stems are the residuals!
04 · MSE

Mean Squared Error (Loss Function)

Some dots are above the line (+5 error) and some are below (−5 error). If we just added them up, they'd cancel out to 0! So we square every error first (5² = 25, (−5)² = 25) and take the average.

Bonus of Squaring: A mistake twice as big gets penalized four times as hard (2² = 4, 4² = 16).
05 · R²

R² Score (Goodness of Fit)

How do we know if our line is actually useful? R-squared (R²) scores your line from 0% to 100% compared to simply guessing the average every time.

Rule of Thumb: 85%+ means a strong linear trend; near 0% means the dots are random scatter.
06 · Pitfalls

Outliers & Overfitting

An Outlier is a freak data point far away from the rest. Because MSE squares errors, a single outlier can yank the whole line toward it! Meanwhile, zig-zagging through every dot is Overfitting (memorizing noise instead of learning the trend).

How the Computer Finds the Best Line: Gradient Descent

Imagine standing blindfolded on a foggy mountain valley where height = total error (MSE) and left/right position = slope weight (w). To reach the lowest point of the valley (minimum error), you feel which way the ground slopes under your feet and take a step downhill. That algorithm is called Gradient Descent.

Step 1 · Random Guess

Start Anywhere on the Hill

The computer starts with a rough guess for the weight (e.g., w = 5 when the true best weight is w = 35). At first, the prediction error is huge, so the ball starts high up on the valley wall.

Step 2 · Measure the Slope (Gradient)

Which Way is Downhill?

The machine calculates the Gradient (the steepness of the error curve at the current spot). If the slope is negative, moving right reduces error; if positive, moving left reduces error.

Step 3 · The Learning Rate Trap

Why "Step Size" Matters So Much

Try dragging the Learning Rate slider on the left!
• Too Small (0.03): Tiny baby steps—takes forever to reach the bottom.
• Just Right (0.18): Reaches the bottom smoothly in 8–10 steps.
• Too High (0.92+): Giant leaps that overshoot across the valley walls back and forth!

Beyond One Line: Multiple Linear Regression

In the real world, rent doesn't depend only on flat size. It also depends on how close the MTR station is and how old the building is! When we combine multiple clues (x₁, x₂, x₃), each with its own weight (w₁, w₂, w₃), it is called Multiple Linear Regression: ŷ = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + w₃x₃ + b.
